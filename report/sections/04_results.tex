\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Landscape Graph Construction}
\label{sec:som-results}

The SOM converged after approximately 150 training epochs on the
400\,000-pixel subsample (80\,\% of 500\,000, with 20\,\% held out
for validation). \Cref{fig:som-convergence} shows the evolution of
validation quantisation error (QE) and topographic error (TE) across
training stages. QE decreases monotonically throughout training,
but the codebook feature maps
(\cref{fig:codebook-comparison}) reveal that this continued
improvement comes at a cost: at 150~epochs, the codebook displays
smooth gradients with distinct regional structure, whereas at
200~epochs the features fragment into noise as the shrinking
neighbourhood radius causes individual neurons to overfit local
samples. While QE continues to decrease beyond 150~epochs, the
topographic error increases and the codebook loses the spatial
coherence needed for meaningful landscape-type clustering. The
final validation metrics (QE\,=\,1.54, TE\,=\,0.14) at 150~epochs mark the 
best trade-off between quantisation fidelity and topological preservation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{som_convergence}
  \caption{SOM convergence on the held-out validation set. Left
  axis: quantisation error (mean $L_2$ distance to best-matching
  unit). Right axis: topographic error (fraction of samples whose
  two closest BMUs are non-adjacent on the grid). The dashed
  vertical line marks the 150-epoch production threshold.}
  \label{fig:som-convergence}
\end{figure}

Hierarchical clustering of the 4\,096 codebook vectors (Ward's
linkage, cut at 30 clusters) partitioned the SOM grid into 30
landscape types representing recurring environmental configurations
across Switzerland. Connected-component labelling and merging of
fragments smaller than 100~pixels produced the final landscape graph
of \textbf{166\,464 patches} and \textbf{465\,833 edges}.

The patch size distribution (\cref{fig:patch-stats}) shows a median
of 340~pixels (${\sim}0.31$\,km$^2$), with a long tail towards
larger contiguous patches in homogeneous terrain (e.g.\ alpine
grassland, dense forest). The minimum enforced size of 100~pixels
(${\sim}0.09$\,km$^2$) ensures that each patch is large enough to
carry reliable feature averages.

The full-extent PCA-coloured patch map (\cref{fig:pca-patches}) reveals
coherent biogeographic structure: similar colours emerge for the
Swiss Plateau and the Po Valley south of the Alps, reflecting
analogous lowland environmental profiles. High-alpine zones form a
distinct cluster, while the southern Pre-Alps and Ticino group with
the Jura arc, both of which are characterised by their southward slope and
intermediate elevations.

\Cref{fig:landscape-roi} zooms into a ${\sim}16 \times 16$\,km
region north of Bern. Urban patches (Bern city centre) are clearly
delineated. The Dählhölzli forest along the Aare, the Schärmenwald
near Ittigen, and the Tiefenau peninsula emerge as distinct
forest-type patches. These structures match local knowledge of the area well.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{patches_bern}
  \caption{Landscape-type map for the Bern region. Colours
  represent the 30 landscape types derived from hierarchical
  clustering of the SOM codebook. The tessellation captures
  transitions between forested hillsides, agricultural plains,
  and built-up areas at 30\,m resolution.}
  \label{fig:landscape-roi}
\end{figure}

The SOM-based discretisation serves a dual purpose: it reduces the
${\sim}$160~million valid pixels to a tractable graph of 166\,464
nodes while ensuring that patch boundaries follow ecological
gradients rather than an arbitrary regular grid. This adaptive
resolution means that patches are smaller in heterogeneous landscapes
(e.g.\ valley floors with interspersed land cover) and larger in
homogeneous terrain (e.g.\ high-alpine meadows), mirroring the
spatial structure relevant to species distributions.

A limitation of the adjacency-based edge construction is that
connections between patches sharing only a single boundary pixel
receive the same weight as connections along extensive shared
borders. The graph therefore contains ``bridge'' edges representing
tenuous spatial contact (e.g.\ two forest patches touching through a
one-pixel corridor under a road). This is revisited in
\cref{sec:limit-detail}.

\subsection{Random Forest Baseline}
\label{sec:rf-results}

A per-species Random Forest served as the non-spatial baseline.
Across 2\,696 species, the RF achieved a mean AUC of 0.874
(median\,=\,0.877), with 99.3\,\% of species exceeding 0.7 and
91.6\,\% exceeding 0.8. Full metric summaries are provided in
\cref{app:rf-baseline}. The RF's key limitation is its
independence assumption: each patch is classified without regard
to its landscape context, so the model cannot distinguish isolated
habitat fragments from patches embedded in connected corridors.

\subsection{Architecture Search}
\label{sec:arch-results}

representative species spanning a range of habitats and
prevalence levels (\cref{tab:arch-search}).  Performance was
measured by validation AUC averaged over the six species.

The extra-wide architecture ({[}64,48,32{]}, 3~layers) yielded the
highest mean AUC (0.809) and was selected for all subsequent
experiments.  Adding a fourth layer did not improve performance,
suggesting that three message-passing hops (covering a
${\sim}$3-patch spatial neighbourhood) capture the relevant
landscape context for Swiss plant species.  This contrasts with
the original \cite{Wu2025} configuration ({[}24,18,8{]}),
which was optimised for global-scale fauna; wider hidden layers
appear necessary to capture the finer environmental gradients
that distinguish Swiss vegetation zones at 30\,m resolution.

\paragraph{Graph structure benefit.}
The improvement from one to three GraphSAGE layers quantifies how
much each species profits from neighbourhood information
propagated through the landscape graph. The largest gains are
observed for alpine and subalpine grassland species (notably
\textit{Potentilla erecta} ($+0.051$ AUC) and \textit{Trifolium
pratense} ($+0.056$)) while the habitat specialist
\textit{Senecio inaequidens} benefits least ($+0.024$), as its
distinct urban niche is already well captured by local features
alone.

\textit{Potentilla erecta} (alpine grassland) provides a
good illustration. This species occupies connected
subalpine meadow patches in distinct elevation bands, shaped by
climate and topography rather than human management. With a
single GraphSAGE layer, predictions rely solely on local patch
features; isolated meadow patches receive unreliable scores
because the model lacks spatial context. With three layers, the
GNN propagates suitability information through adjacent patches,
correctly identifying connected alpine grassland belts as
suitable habitat.

\subsection{SOM-GNN-SDM Performance}
\label{sec:gnn-results}

The production GNN-SDM was trained for all 2,696 qualifying
species using the extra-wide GraphSAGE architecture
({[}64,48,32{]}). Training completed in 5.7~hours of GPU time
(mean 7.6\,s and 231~epochs per species). Early stopping
triggered for 90.4\,\% of species before reaching the 500-epoch
maximum (\cref{app:gnn-training}).

\paragraph{Discrimination.}
Across all 2,696 species, the GNN-SDM achieved a mean evaluation
AUC of \textbf{0.912} (median\,=\,0.923, $\sigma$\,=\,0.055),
with 90.4\,\% of species exceeding 0.85. The validation AUC used
for early stopping averaged 0.886, with 2,437 species
(90.4\,\%) above~0.8 and only 14 species below~0.6.

\paragraph{TSS and classification skill.}
The True Skill Statistic (defined as
sensitivity~$+$~specificity~$-$~1 and independent of
prevalence) provides a threshold-dependent measure of
classification accuracy. At the threshold maximising TSS, the
GNN-SDM achieved a mean TSS of \textbf{0.583}
($\Delta$\,=\,+0.153 over RF). This indicates that the model
correctly identifies both presence and absence patches well
above chance, despite the extreme class imbalance (median
species prevalence ${\sim}$0.8\,\% of patches).

\paragraph{Comparison with RF baseline.}
\Cref{tab:gnn-rf} summarises the head-to-head comparison.  The
GNN-SDM outperforms the RF on every metric, with the largest
gains in recall ($+$0.137), F1 ($+$0.126), and TSS ($+$0.153).
On a per-species basis, the GNN achieves higher AUC than the RF
for \textbf{2,299 of 2,696 species (85.3\,\%)}.

Species with spatially structured distributions benefit most from neighbourhood
aggregation, while widespread generalists show smaller gains
because their habitat signal is already well captured by local
features alone. The RF remains a strong baseline
(AUC\,=\,0.874): the 12 selected features carry strong predictive power even 
without spatial context. The GNN primarily improves predictions at patch 
boundaries and in fragmented landscapes.

\textit{Potentilla erecta} (\cref{fig:potentilla}) illustrates
the difference: the GNN produces sharp habitat boundaries along
connected alpine grassland belts, while the RF assigns diffuse
moderate scores across the entire elevation band.

\paragraph{Common vs.\ threatened species.}
A detailed evaluation on 12 selected species (6~common,
6~vulnerable) shows no systematic performance gap: vulnerable
species achieve a mean AUC of 0.874 and TSS of 0.708, compared
to 0.863 and 0.604 for common species
(\cref{fig:rare-common}). The slightly higher scores for
vulnerable species reflect their narrower ecological niches,
which are easier to discriminate from the landscape background.

\subsection{Conservation Prioritisation}
\label{sec:conservation-results}

The circuit-theory connectivity analysis was applied to 285
nationally threatened species (Swiss Red List categories CR,
EN, VU, NT) with $\geq 50$ presence patches. After excluding
44 species with insufficient connectivity structure (zero mean
current), 241 species (2~CR, 17~EN, 77~VU, 145~NT; 358
weight-units) contributed to the multi-species priority surface.

\paragraph{Protection baseline.}
Restricting the analysis to the 65,298 patches within Swiss
territory, the current protected-area network covers
28.6\,\% of patches: 1.7\,\% under strict protection
(IUCN categories Ia--III) and 21.4\,\% under managed
protection (categories IV--VI). The remaining 71.4\,\%
(46,603 patches) lack any formal protection status. Note that
this analysis considers a patch as protected if at least one
of its constituent pixels overlaps with a protected area
polygon---a generous criterion that may overstate actual
coverage.

\paragraph{Gap analysis.}
The priority surface, combining threat-weighted suitability
and connectivity, identifies patches that are simultaneously
important habitat and critical corridor links for threatened
species. \Cref{fig:gap-ch} shows the national-scale priority
map overlaid with existing protected areas.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{gap_ch}
  \caption{Multi-species conservation priority surface for
  Switzerland, aggregated across 241 nationally threatened
  species. High-priority patches (warm colours) combine high
  GNN-predicted habitat suitability with critical
  circuit-theory connectivity. Existing protected areas are
  outlined.}
  \label{fig:gap-ch}
\end{figure}

Among the top-5\,\% priority patches (3,265 patches):
\begin{itemize}
  \item 58.9\,\% fall within some protected area
    (4.2\,\% strictly, 43.5\,\% managed, 11.2\,\% with
    unclassified IUCN category),
  \item \textbf{41.1\,\% (1,342 patches) are entirely
    unprotected}: these represent the primary conservation
    gaps.
\end{itemize}

Focusing on the top-1\,\% critical corridor patches (653
patches), protection coverage improves slightly: 6.4\,\%
strict, 58.3\,\% managed, 9.5\,\% unclassified, but
\textbf{25.7\,\% (168 patches) remain unprotected}. These 168 patches represent strong
connectivity links between isolated populations of threatened
species that currently have no formal protection.

\paragraph{Regional patterns.}
The spatial distribution of conservation gaps is not uniform
across Switzerland. \Cref{fig:gap-region} illustrates four
contrasting regions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Engadin:} strong alignment between existing
    protected areas and priority patches: the extensive
    national park and surrounding reserves effectively cover
    critical connectivity corridors.
  \item \textbf{Seeland:} poor match: high-priority wetland
    and agricultural transition patches largely fall outside
    existing protection, despite their importance for
    threatened lowland flora.
  \item \textbf{Les Franches-Montagnes:} partial overlap:
    some priority grassland corridors are captured by
    landscape protection areas, but key connecting patches
    remain unprotected.
  \item \textbf{Surselva (west of Chur):} notable
    mismatch: several protected areas in this region do not
    coincide with high-priority patches, while critical
    corridors identified by the model lie outside existing
    designations.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{gap_region}
  \caption{Regional detail of four contrasting areas showing
  the alignment (or mismatch) between the priority surface
  and existing protected areas. Engadin shows good coverage,
  while Seeland and Surselva reveal significant conservation
  gaps where high-priority habitat lacks protection.}
  \label{fig:gap-region}
\end{figure}

The gap analysis reveals two categories of conservation action:
(1)~168 unprotected critical corridor patches requiring new
protection designation, and (2)~381 patches under managed
protection (category~IV--VI) that may warrant upgrading to
stricter status given their connectivity importance. The
finding that 41\,\% of high-priority habitat lacks any
protection aligns with the broader assessment that
Switzerland's ecological infrastructure remains incomplete
\citep{FOEN2025}.

The regional heterogeneity shows that protection
effectiveness cannot be assessed by area alone. The Surselva
example shows that protected areas optimised for landscape
aesthetics or recreation do not necessarily capture the
ecological corridors critical for threatened plant species.
Conversely, the Engadin demonstrates that well-placed
protection can effectively cover connectivity needs.
