\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limit-detail}

\paragraph{GBIF sampling bias.}
GBIF occurrence data is subject to strong spatial,
temporal, and taxonomic biases. Observations cluster near
roads, trails, and population centres; remote alpine areas
and private land are systematically under-sampled. The
model interprets absence from the database as
pseudo-absence, which conflates true absence with lack of
sampling effort. While this is standard practice in SDM
and the overprediction of suitable habitat is a
desirable fail-safe for conservation, it means predicted
ranges should not be interpreted as precise distribution
boundaries without field verification.

\paragraph{No independent field validation.}
Model performance is evaluated against held-out GBIF data,
which shares the same sampling biases as the training set.
Independent validation against systematically collected
field surveys (e.g.\ Info Flora monitoring plots) would
provide a stronger assessment of predictive accuracy,
particularly for rare species in under-sampled regions.

\paragraph{Temporal snapshot.}
All environmental features and occurrence records represent
a single temporal snapshot (climate normals 1981--2010,
land cover 2021, GBIF records up to 2024). The model does
not account for ongoing climate change, land-use
intensification, or species range shifts. Priority areas
identified today may lose relevance under future climate
scenarios.

\paragraph{SOM patch size heterogeneity.}
The SOM-based discretisation produces patches that are
adaptive in size, but the adaptation is imperfect. In
homogeneous high-alpine terrain, patches can grow very
large ($>$1000 pixels, ${\sim}$0.9\,km$^2$), merging what
may be ecologically distinct micro-habitats (e.g.\
snow-bed communities vs.\ wind-exposed ridges). Ideally,
large alpine patches should be subdivided based on
additional criteria such as aspect or micro-topography.
This coarsening reduces spatial precision for alpine
specialists.

\paragraph{Unweighted edges.}
The landscape graph uses binary adjacency: patches sharing
at least one boundary pixel are connected with equal
weight. This treats a single-pixel corridor under a road
identically to a broad shared border between two forest
patches. Edge weighting by shared-boundary length or by a
resistance surface would better represent functional
connectivity, particularly for the circuit-theory
analysis where current should preferentially flow through
wide corridors.

\paragraph{SOM quantisation loss.}
Assigning each 30\,m pixel to its best-matching unit and
then averaging features within patches introduces
quantisation loss. Fine-scale environmental gradients
within a patch (e.g.\ a south-facing rock outcrop within
a north-facing forest patch) are smoothed away. This is
the trade-off enabling graph-based modelling
at national scale, but it limits predictions for species
whose distributions are driven by micro-habitat variation.

\paragraph{Protected-area overlay simplification.}
The gap analysis treats protection as binary (any pixel
overlap counts) and does not differentiate management
effectiveness. A patch overlapping a category-IV landscape
protection area receives the same ``protected'' label as
one within a strict nature reserve, though actual
conservation outcomes may differ substantially.

\subsection{Future Directions}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{Climate scenario projections.}
Replacing static climate normals with CHELSA future
projections (SSP scenarios) would enable forecasting of
range shifts and identification of climate refugia.
The pipeline architecture supports this directly:
recomputing patch features under a future climate scenario
and re-running the trained GNN yields projected
suitability surfaces without retraining.

\paragraph{Edge-weighted graph.}
Replacing binary adjacency with resistance-based edge
weights (derived from land-cover permeability or
shared-boundary length) would improve the ecological
realism of both the GNN message passing and the
circuit-theory connectivity analysis.

\paragraph{Multi-species GNN.}
The current per-species training is computationally
simple but cannot exploit correlations between species
with similar habitat requirements. A joint multi-species
architecture (e.g.\ species embeddings concatenated with
patch features) could improve predictions for rare species
by transferring information from ecologically similar
common species.

\paragraph{Adaptive patch subdivision.}
Introducing a secondary subdivision step for large
homogeneous patches (splitting by aspect, elevation
band, or micro-topographic position) would improve
spatial precision in alpine terrain without affecting the
lowland patches that are already well-resolved.

\paragraph{Transfer to other regions.}
The pipeline is not specific to Switzerland: given
environmental rasters and occurrence data, the full
pipeline can be applied to any study area. Transfer to
neighbouring Alpine countries (Austria, northern Italy)
or to other taxa (insects, birds) would test the
generalisability of the approach.
